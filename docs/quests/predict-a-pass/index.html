Lesson 9 of 11 · 10 min · Orbits and timing

Predict a space station pass

Use orbit data to find when the International Space Station next crosses your sky, and where to look.

Pick your level

On paper, write the lesson title and your level. Number your notes to match the steps. Tick each one when you finish it.

Steps for beginner crews

  1. Open Passes. Type the latitude and longitude of a nearby town centre, then press Use this location.
  2. Press the button to download orbit data for the space station.
  3. Look at the next pass. Write down when it rises, which direction it rises from, and how high it gets.

Check your work

You have a time, a compass direction and a highest angle in degrees for one pass.

Talk it over

If a pass is just after sunset, you might see the station as a bright dot moving across the sky. Why can't you see it in the middle of the day?

Steps for intermediate crews

  1. Copy the passes for the next 48 hours into a table with rise time, highest angle and duration in minutes.
  2. Find the time between the start of one pass and the start of the next.
  3. Look for a pattern between highest angle and duration.

Check your work

Your table has every pass listed, and you have written the pattern in one sentence.

Talk it over

The station goes around Earth about every 92 minutes. Why don't you get a pass every 92 minutes?

Steps for professional crews

  1. Note the age of the orbit data the app shows. Then refresh it and see whether the times move.
  2. On a computer with the ground station, run cargo run --locked -- passes --tle amateur.tle --satellite ISS --lat <lat> --lon <lon> --hours 48 with the same coordinates (see quest/m0/predict-a-pass.md for getting the orbit file).
  3. Compare the two lists, pass by pass, in seconds.

Check your work

You have a side-by-side table with the difference for each pass and a reason for any difference over a minute.

Talk it over

Both tools use SGP4. List what could still make them disagree, and what could make both of them wrong.
